Hearthstone · thought experiment

Ban One Class

What if every ladder player could pick one class they never get matched against? This page replays the current meta under that rule, using real matchup win rates, and lets the population adapt until it settles.

TL;DR

  • It reins in outlier decks. From Diamond 4 up, the most played deck loses a quarter to a third of its players (Zoo Warlock 24% → 17% in Legend, 30% → 19% in Top 1K) and its win rate drops 1–3 points. The gap between the best and worst deck shrinks, from 21 to 15.5 points in Legend.
  • Banning your counter doesn't make a deck unbeatable. The biggest winners are weak decks finally dodging their worst matchup (Mill Rogue and Ayaya Rogue gain 6–7 points on day one in Legend). A mid-tier deck can use its ban to climb: C'Thun Warlock bans Warlock to skip Zoo and rises from 48.7% to about 53% in Top 1K. It ends up no stronger than Zoo is today, because everyone else has a ban too.
  • More decks are viable, but popularity doesn't spread much. Decks at 50%+ win rate go up by 2–3 in Diamond 4 and above. The number of decks played by 5%+ stays about the same: Zoo's lost players spread across many mid-sized decks.
  • You see the top deck far less often, but not more different decks. In Legend, Zoo falls from 24% of your games to 12%, and the three most common decks from 47% to 39%. Your own ban removes a whole class from your queue, so the number of different decks you meet stays about the same.
  • The effect grows with rank. Platinum and Diamond 10–5 barely move, because those players don't chase win rate and ban less (25–31% ban Warlock). Top 1K players chase win rate hardest, so their meta shifts most. In a meta with no dominant deck (#359), the effect is small everywhere except Top 1K.

Figures are for the #360 snapshot at default settings unless stated. The rest of the page updates live when you change snapshot, bracket or knobs.

How it works

  • Snapshots. Each snapshot is one Vicious Syndicate Data Reaper report. #360 (Oct 21–26 2026) is the first week of Reign of the Black Empire, before the meta has settled. #359 (Sep 16–22 2026) is the last report before it, from the previous expansion's more established meta. Comparing them shows how much the ban effect depends on how lopsided the meta is.
  • Matchups. Every head-to-head win rate between the most played archetypes, read from that report's All Ranks matchup chart. Fringe lists are left out. A pair vS didn't chart (under 100 games) is treated as 50/50.
  • Per-bracket data. Each bracket's deck win rates and relative play rates come from the vS Power Rankings for that bracket. The All Ranks matchup grid is nudged per bracket (a strength offset per deck) so every deck's overall win rate matches what vS reports there. That stands in for better piloting and tighter lists at higher ranks.
  • How picky each bracket is. Within a bracket, the model reads how much more a deck is played for each point of win rate (a log-linear fit of play rate on win rate). Everything else about a deck's popularity (cost, fun, familiarity) is held fixed as that deck's appeal. Without bans, the model reproduces today's meta exactly. With bans, win rates change, and each bracket reacts as strongly as its own data says it does.
  • The ban rule. Each player picks one class before queueing. Two players are matched only if neither has banned the other's class. Banning your own class (skipping mirrors) is allowed. Players weigh bans by the win rate each would give them, with the same pickiness as their bracket (adjustable below).
  • Left out. Deck tuning and new archetypes, queue times, and the balance patch vS expects. vS doesn't publish Bronze–Gold separately, so Platinum is the most casual bracket here. Treat the output as a direction, not a forecast.
